What probing buys

An observer watches someone play poker and tries to work out what they are trying to do. It can sit back and watch, or it can probe — make a move that forces the subject to answer something. This is what a probe is, what it buys, and where it turns against you.

01

What a probe actually is

A probe is the observer making a move that forces the subject into a decision they would otherwise never face — and choosing that move so their answer splits the intent set as far as possible.

In Leduc the subject makes two decisions per hand on their own: they open each betting round. A passive observer checks and calls through, so those two moves are all it ever sees. It never asks the subject anything. Betting or raising creates a third decision — fold, call, or raise — that did not exist before.

This is the cleanest isolation of what “adaptive” buys. Random and adaptive force nearly the same number of answers — 1,569 against 1,530. Holding the act of probing constant, each adaptive answer cuts 1.39 intents against random's 1.22. Same number of questions; better-chosen ones. That residual is the selection rule and nothing else.

A confirming signal sits in where the information comes from. The share of elimination contributed by the showdown card reveal — pure watching, not asking — is 21% for the passive observer but only 9% for the adaptive one. The adaptive observer has shifted its information source from watching to asking.

Only trap checks a jack and then calls a bet. That fact is invisible unless someone bets. The observer spends a chip to manufacture the one situation in which trap is obliged to distinguish itself — a counterfactual made actual. Checking would have revealed the card and nothing about what the subject was trying to do.

02

Probing works

Choosing moves to expose intent roughly triples exact identification in Kuhn and adds ten points in Leduc — against a subject whose behaviour the intent model actually describes.

Three observers see identical deals. passive never probes: it checks and calls through. random acts arbitrarily. adaptive picks the move that minimises the expected number of intents left standing. Nothing else differs.

The other half of the same result is the surviving set. Adaptive probing cuts it from 2.85 to 2.06 intents in Kuhn and 3.16 to 2.65 in Leduc — our working proxy for worst-case distinctiveness. random is the control that matters: acting arbitrarily gets you a little in Kuhn and loses ground in Leduc. Acting is not what helps. Acting on the right rule is.

03

And it is free

Probing looked like it cost half a chip a hand. Almost all of that was waste: a small chip-awareness term buys back the loss and identifies slightly better.

In Leduc the information-only observer loses −0.509 chips a hand, because a round-two probe is a four-chip bet. Weighting its objective by μ — minimise expected intents left, minus μ times expected chips — traces the frontier between knowing and winning.

At μ = 0.1 the observer identifies 39.0% — marginally better than the information-only 38.5% — while turning −0.509 chips a hand into +0.147. The loss was never the price of information. It was probes that bought nothing the observer could not get more cheaply. Beyond that the frontier bends: μ = 1 gives 30.0% at +0.652, and by μ ≥ 2 you have a chip maximiser that happens to learn things.

This moves the work toward goal recognition design rather than away from it. GRD minimises worst-case distinctiveness subject to not preventing agents from achieving their goals — a constrained optimisation. μ is that constraint.

04

Until the vocabulary doesn't fit

Everything above holds only while the subject's behaviour is generated by one of the intents we wrote down. When it isn't, probing stops helping — and then starts actively hurting.

Goal recognition design assumes the actor's behaviour comes from one of the modelled goals. Real subjects are not in that set — rarely because they conceal, mostly because a finite menu of intents will not cover what a person actually does. They pick the nearest label, change their mind, misread the option, or play badly. All of it looks identical to the observer.

First the advantage simply evaporates. Against behaviour the model cannot explain, exact identification falls to 0% for every observer, probing or not. That is survivable: the observer ends up uncertain, which is honest.

The turn

Now consider a subject who declares one intent and simply plays a different one — faithfully, never contradicting the model. The nearest-option case. There is no deception here at all.

Because the played intent generates every action and matches the revealed card, it can never be eliminated. So whenever the observer narrows to a single intent, that intent is the one being played — and the declared one is wrong. Probing harder makes the observer more certain of the wrong answer.

In Kuhn the ordering is unambiguous: the adaptive observer is misattributed 79.2% of the time against passive's 25.5%. In Leduc every observer is badly wrong and the adaptive one is worst, at 98.6%; the random observer is the safest of the three purely because it learns least. Competence is the vulnerability.

And the richer game is the worse one — 98.6% against Kuhn's 79.2%. More room to distinguish intents means intents are more distinguishable, which is exactly what makes the wrong one identifiable with certainty. Every gain in worst-case distinctiveness is also a gain in worst-case mis-distinctiveness.

Where this leaves us

Probing is the right rule, and it is not enough

The observer in act 3 is not being lied to. It is being told the truth by someone whose label and behaviour have come apart, and it answers with precision. Nothing in the elimination framework fixes this: the conclusion is correct about the policy and wrong only about the name.

On the literature

Everything above is online inquiry inside a fixed environment: the observer chooses its move, it cannot redesign the game. Goal recognition design does the stronger thing — it modifies the environment in advance so that whichever goal an agent holds becomes evident sooner. A companion gridworld now implements both and reports them separately, which is what lets us make an actual GRD claim rather than an analogy.

The open problem

No action history separates “is executing probe” from “believes they are executing value_bet.” That is a limit of behavioural inference, not of this implementation — and it is the strongest argument for measuring declared intent against behaviour rather than assuming they match.

What we can already do

When behaviour leaves the model entirely, the observer can detect it and abstain, trading four to five confident-wrong answers for each confident-right one given up — and costing nothing at all against a faithful subject.

What comes next

A tolerance term so a single off-policy human action does not empty the hypothesis set, then a human pilot to measure how often real declared intent and real behaviour actually diverge.

Exact set elimination over a finite hypothesis space — no LLM, no learned distance metric, no sampling where enumeration is possible. Every figure reproduces from sweep.py; conditions are compared on identical deals, so differences are paired rather than independent samples.
